EVIDENCE SUPPORT: SUPPORTING DATA FOR THE PUBLISHED THREADS (E1–E7)
Added October 2026. Separate file, so it can be reviewed, edited or removed on its own.

HOW TO USE THIS FILE
- These items strengthen confidence in the existing threads. They are NOT extra multipliers:
  the published calculation (72–93%) does not change because of them. Never multiply them in.
- Cite the source when you use an item, and state the consensus level honestly.
- Never overclaim. If a point is debated, say so, then give the reply.
- Each entry has the shape of the planned public evidence ledger: claim, source, consensus,
  which thread it supports, strongest objection, reply.

---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
S1. PAUL'S OWN FIRST-HAND CLAIM (supports E5, Paul)
Claim: Paul says in his own letters that he saw the risen Jesus.
Source: 1 Corinthians 9:1 ("Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?"); 1 Corinthians 15:8; Galatians 1:15-16.
Dating: 1 Corinthians c. AD 53-55; Galatians c. AD 48-55. Both are among the seven letters
  accepted as Paul's own by nearly all critical scholars.
Consensus: near-universal that these are Paul's words and that Paul believed he had seen Jesus.
Strongest objection: Paul's experience was a vision (Acts describes a light and a voice), so it
  shows nothing about a body.
Reply: Paul puts his experience in the same list as the group appearances, as "last of all".
  E5 measures the change from persecutor to witness, which needs a cause whatever the nature of
  the experience. Our ratios already allow for visionary experiences under the alternative.

S2. WHERE THE CREED CAME FROM (supports E3, early creed)
Claim: The creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 was handed on to Paul as tradition, and Paul met named
  witnesses in Jerusalem within a few years of his conversion.
Source: 1 Corinthians 15:3 uses the standard terms for passing on tradition ("delivered...
  received"); Galatians 1:18-19: Paul stayed fifteen days with Peter ("Cephas") and saw James,
  three years after his conversion.
Dating: Paul's conversion is usually placed c. AD 31-36, so the Jerusalem visit falls in the mid
  to late 30s.
Consensus: majority of critical scholars, including sceptical ones, date the creed's origin to
  within a few years of the crucifixion.
Strongest objection: Paul could have composed the creed himself.
Reply: its wording is unusual for Paul, it uses the Aramaic name "Cephas", and Paul explicitly
  calls it something he received. Galatians ties it to Peter and James personally.

S3. VERY EARLY WORSHIP OF JESUS (supports E7, novel belief)
Claim: Within about two decades, Jewish monotheists prayed to Jesus and sang about him as Lord.
Source: 1 Corinthians 16:22, "Maranatha" (Aramaic, "Our Lord, come"), an Aramaic prayer kept
  untranslated in a Greek letter, which points to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers;
  Philippians 2:6-11, a hymn widely regarded as earlier than Paul.
Scholarship: Larry Hurtado, "Lord Jesus Christ" (2003); Richard Bauckham, "Jesus and the God of
  Israel" (2008).
Consensus: that devotion to Jesus began very early is the majority view; that Philippians 2 is
  pre-Pauline is widely held but debated.
Strongest objection: Philippians 2 may be Paul's own composition.
Reply: even then it is written in the 50s or early 60s to a church expected to share it, and
  "Maranatha" is independent evidence of earlier Aramaic devotion. Even sceptical scholars such as
  Bart Ehrman ("How Jesus Became God", 2014) accept that exalted beliefs about Jesus arose very
  early, while explaining them differently.

S4. THE SCANDAL OF A CRUCIFIED MESSIAH (supports E7)
Claim: Followers did not invent a convenient hero. A crucified messiah was a scandal they had to
  explain, not a story anyone would choose.
Source: 1 Corinthians 1:23 ("a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles");
  Galatians 3:13 citing Deuteronomy 21:23 (one hung on a tree is cursed).
Consensus: the crucifixion is among the most certain facts about Jesus, accepted across the field
  (for example by John Dominic Crossan and Bart Ehrman); its embarrassment is widely recognised.
Strongest objection: embarrassing details can be invented to seem authentic.
Reply: the crucifixion is confirmed by non-Christian sources (see S5); early Christians had to
  defend it at length rather than exploit it.

S5. NON-CHRISTIAN CONFIRMATION OF THE STARTING FACTS (supports the baseline, and E6, James)
Claim: Roman and Jewish sources confirm the crucifixion under Pilate and the execution of James,
  the brother of Jesus.
Source: Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (c. AD 116): Christ suffered the death penalty under Tiberius, at
  the hands of the procurator Pontius Pilate. Josephus, Antiquities 20.200 (c. AD 93): James,
  "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ", was stoned in AD 62.
Consensus: Tacitus's passage is accepted as genuine by nearly all historians; the James passage in
  Josephus is accepted as genuine by most (unlike Josephus's longer passage on Jesus, which was
  partly added to by later Christian copyists, and which we do not rely on).
Strongest objection: Tacitus may only repeat what Christians said.
Reply: possibly, but it shows the crucifixion under Pilate was known in Rome as fact, and Josephus
  is an independent Jewish source for James.

S6. NO JEWISH CATEGORY FOR ONE MAN RISING EARLY (supports E7)
Claim: Second Temple Jewish hope looked for a bodily resurrection of all the righteous at the end
  of the age, not one individual raised in the middle of history. The Christian claim reshaped
  that belief in specific ways that need a cause.
Scholarship: N. T. Wright, "The Resurrection of the Son of God" (2003), a detailed survey of
  ancient Jewish and pagan beliefs about life after death.
Consensus: Wright's survey of the background is widely used across the field; his conclusion (that
  the resurrection is the best explanation) is contested.
Strongest objection: Wright is a believer, so his reading is biased.
Reply: the survey of what ancient Jews believed is checked against the primary sources and is
  used by scholars who reject his conclusion. Our E7 ratio (3) is deliberately modest and does not
  depend on his conclusion.
